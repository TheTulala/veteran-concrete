Repair before you replace

Tearing out concrete is expensive, loud, and usually unnecessary

Replacing a slab means demolition, hauling, a new base, a new pour, and days of a yard you can't use. Most of the time the concrete underneath is fine. It's only the surface that looks bad.

Sun-bleached color, rust and oil stains, flaking from a rushed finish, old patches that never matched, or a slab that was simply poured plain gray and never touched again. All of those are surface problems, and surface problems have surface solutions.

Fixing substandard concrete without the hassle and high cost of removing it is a big part of what Veteran Concrete does. Foreman Rick Kaylor, our coatings technician and mix expert, helps decide whether your slab can be saved, and the same crew does the work.

Is your slab a good overlay candidate?

  • Usually yesHairline cracks, faded or blotchy color, oil and rust stains, flaking or rough spots, dated plain gray, a poor finish from a past contractor.
  • Usually noSinking or heaving sections, wide cracks with one side higher than the other, crumbling concrete, or water pooling against the house.

Resurfacing options

Concrete staining, overlays, and coatings

The right finish depends on what shape the slab is in and how you use it. Here's what we work with.

  • Acid stain

    Reacts with the concrete for rich, marbled, one-of-a-kind earth tones. Best on sound concrete with a clean surface.

  • Water-based stain

    A wider color range with more even, predictable results. Great for refreshing faded patios and walks.

  • Stamped overlay

    A new layer stamped with stone, slate, or wood plank patterns, so an old slab gets the stamped concrete look.

  • Knockdown finish

    A sprayed and troweled texture that adds grip and stays cooler in light colors. A pool deck staple.

  • Microtopping

    A thin, smooth cement skin for a clean modern look on patios, entries, and interior floors.

  • Concrete coatings

    Tough protective coatings for garages, shops, and commercial floors that need to take real wear.

Built for the desert

Concrete resurfacing that stands up to Las Vegas

  • Pool decks that burn

    Dark, smooth concrete gets painfully hot by noon. A light knockdown overlay is cooler underfoot and grips when wet.

  • Hard water spots

    Sprinkler overspray leaves white mineral rings on concrete. A good sealer helps the surface resist them and clean up easier.

  • Hot garage floors

    Summer heat and hot tires lift cheap garage coatings. Proper prep and the right coating system keep it bonded.

How it works

How we resurface a concrete slab

  1. Inspect the slab

    We check cracks, movement, drainage, and the surface to make sure an overlay or stain will actually last.

  2. Repair and prep

    Cracks are repaired, and the surface is ground and cleaned so the new layer bonds instead of peeling.

  3. Overlay and color

    The overlay goes down troweled, stamped, or sprayed, then gets stained or tinted to the color you picked.

  4. Seal and cure

    A protective sealer locks in the color. We walk it with you and tell you when it's ready for traffic.

Can you put an overlay over cracked concrete?

Yes, if the cracks are stable. Hairline and minor cracks are repaired and filled before the overlay goes down. Wide, shifting, or displaced cracks mean the slab is moving underneath, and an overlay will crack right along with it, so that concrete should be replaced instead.

How long does a concrete overlay last?

A well-prepared, properly bonded, and sealed overlay lasts for many years. Surface prep is what makes or breaks it, and resealing every few years in the Las Vegas sun keeps it looking new.

Should I stain my concrete or overlay it?

Stain changes the color of a slab whose surface is still in good shape. An overlay builds a brand new surface, so it's the better choice when the concrete is rough, patched, or flaking, or when you want a stamped pattern or texture.

Will an overlay fix low spots or drainage problems?

Only small ones. An overlay can smooth minor dips, but it can't correct a slab that slopes toward the house or holds standing water. Those problems usually need the section removed and repoured with the right slope.
